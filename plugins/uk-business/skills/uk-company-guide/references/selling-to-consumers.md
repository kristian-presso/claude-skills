# Selling to consumers in the UK

> Reference, not legal, tax or accounting advice. Figures researched against the official sources listed at the end on 06/10/2026; key figures re-checked on gov.uk on 16/10/2026. Confidence: high. Re-check by 06/01/2027, or sooner after a Budget or Spring Statement.

For UK founders selling goods, digital content or services to the public, in person or online. Three laws do most of the work: the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (what customers are entitled to), the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 (extra rules for online, phone and off-premises sales) and the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (unfair practices and CMA fines). This is reference material, not legal advice. B2B sales follow different rules.

## Consumer Rights Act 2015: what customers are entitled to

| You sell | Must be | If it is not |
|---|---|---|
| Goods | Of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose, as described, matching any sample or model | Up to 30 days: reject for a full refund. After that: one repair or replacement, then a price reduction or final rejection |
| Digital content (apps, downloads, streaming) | Satisfactory quality, fit for purpose, as described | Repair or replacement, then a price reduction. If it damages the customer's device or other content through lack of reasonable care, you must repair or compensate |
| Services | Performed with reasonable care and skill; anything you said that the customer relied on is binding | Repeat performance, or a price reduction if that is impossible or not done in reasonable time |

Key timings for goods:

- **30-day short-term right to reject.** Runs from the day after the customer owns the goods, has them delivered and (if needed) has had them installed.
- **First 6 months.** A fault is presumed to have existed at sale unless you prove otherwise. No deduction for use if the customer finally rejects in this period, except for motor vehicles.
- **After 6 months.** The customer may need to show the fault was there at the time of sale.
- **Claims** can be brought up to 6 years later (5 in Scotland).
- **Refunds after a rejection:** without undue delay and within 14 days of agreeing the customer is entitled, by the same payment method, with no fee.

Sale items carry the same rights. A "no refunds" sign that hides statutory rights is illegal. A warranty or guarantee adds to these rights and never replaces them.

## Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013: online and distance sales

### Before the customer orders, tell them clearly and in a form they can keep

- who you are, your address and email
- what they are buying
- the total price including taxes, and delivery costs
- how to pay and delivery arrangements
- minimum contract length and how to end it
- the cancellation right, when it is lost, and a model cancellation form

Online you must also:

- show the order steps and let customers correct errors
- label the button so it is clear they must pay (for example "pay now")
- get active consent to optional extras
- give your VAT number if registered

Confirm the contract by email no later than delivery or the start of the service. Deliver goods within 30 days unless you agreed otherwise.

### 14-day cancellation ("cooling off")

| Contract | Cancellation window | Refund deadline |
|---|---|---|
| Goods | Ends 14 days after the customer receives them | 14 days after you get the goods back, or after proof of return if earlier |
| Services | Ends 14 days after the contract is made | 14 days after you are told of the cancellation |
| Digital content (not on a disc) | Ends 14 days after the contract is made | As for services |

- You refund the original standard delivery charge. If they chose a pricier option, you only refund the standard rate.
- You can deduct for diminished value only if the goods were handled beyond what a shop would allow.
- If you never told them about the cancellation right, the window extends by up to 12 months.

**Services started early.** You may start within the 14 days only if the customer expressly asks (on a durable medium for off-premises deals). If they then cancel, they pay a proportionate amount for what was supplied. Once the service is fully performed after such a request, the right to cancel is lost.

**Digital content.** Before download or streaming starts, get the customer's express consent and their acknowledgement that they lose the right to cancel. Repeat this in your confirmation. Skip this and they keep the 14-day right without paying.

### No cancellation right (common exceptions)

- made-to-measure or personalised goods
- goods that deteriorate quickly
- sealed hygiene goods once unsealed
- sealed audio, video or software once unsealed
- newspapers and magazines (not subscriptions)
- accommodation, transport of goods, car hire, catering and leisure services for a specific date or period, such as event tickets
- off-premises contracts of £42 or less

## DMCC Act 2024: unfair practices and CMA fines

From 06/04/2025 the DMCC Act replaced the old unfair trading regulations. Most of the old rules carry over, with these big changes:

- **CMA direct enforcement.** The CMA can now decide a breach itself and fine, without going to court. Penalties are up to the higher of £300,000 or 10% of worldwide turnover. Most banned practices are also criminal offences (fine and up to 2 years in prison).
- **32 practices are banned outright**, whatever their effect. These include fake reviews, misusing trust marks and pressure selling.
- **Fake reviews.** You must not write, commission or publish fake reviews, or reviews that hide an incentive, and you must not present reviews misleadingly. If you publish reviews, take reasonable and proportionate steps to prevent and remove fake ones. A practical baseline: a published review policy, a check that the reviewer actually bought, labelled incentivised reviews, and no cherry-picking.
- **Drip pricing is prohibited.** Do not show a headline price and then add mandatory charges as the customer moves through checkout. Any fee every buyer must pay belongs in the price shown up front. The CMA's price transparency guidance (CMA209) covers how to present optional extras and delivery.
- **Material information.** Any "invitation to purchase" (shop label, product page, basket, menu, social ad, promo email) must show the total price, main characteristics, your identity and address, and cancellation rights. Leaving them out is unfair regardless of its effect on customers.
- **Coming next.** On 09/08/2026 the government said it will consult this autumn on banning fake "was" prices, invented discounts and misleading RRPs. Check [changes ledger](changes-ledger.md).

## Subscription contracts: status

The DMCC Act's subscription regime is not yet in force. On 09/08/2026 the government announced it will start in January 2027 (earlier than the previously expected spring 2027). No exact day was given in that announcement. When it applies, businesses must:

- give clearer up-front information
- send regular reminders
- make cancelling easy
- offer a new 14-day cooling-off period after a free or discounted trial ends and after a long contract auto-renews

Some charitable cultural and heritage memberships will be excluded. If you sell memberships or auto-renewing plans, design the reminder and easy-exit flow now.

## Price display and terms basics

- Give consumers the total price including all taxes. If you are VAT registered, see [vat registration and thresholds](vat-registration-and-thresholds.md).
- Discounts must be genuine. Do not inflate a "was" price.
- Write terms in plain, legible language. Under the Consumer Rights Act an unfair term or notice is not binding on the consumer. Terms setting the price and main subject matter escape the fairness test only if they are transparent and prominent.
- Never use terms that limit statutory rights. Include your business identity, delivery, returns and complaints details.
- You can ask for proof of purchase, but a receipt, bank statement or packaging can all count.
