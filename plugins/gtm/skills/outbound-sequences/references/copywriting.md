# Writing the touches

The job of an outreach message is to earn one reply, not to explain the product. Every
rule below pushes towards shorter, sharper and easier to answer.

## Length

- First touch: about **50 to 125 words**. The best-performing senders reportedly average
  under 80.
- Messages over about 200 words get fewer replies. Cut anything that is not relevance,
  proof or the ask.
- Follow-ups can be shorter still. Two or three sentences is often enough.

## Subject lines

- Under about **50 characters** so they are not cut off on a phone.
- Two or three words, lower case, conversational. It should read like a note from a
  colleague, not a campaign.
- Never fake a reply ("Re:") or a forward ("Fwd:"). It is dishonest and recipients
  punish it.

## What personalisation actually is

- **Not** a first name or company name dropped into a merge field. Readers do not notice
  it and it does not move reply rates.
- **Is** one specific, accurate observation that proves this message was written for
  this person: a sentence they could not receive in anyone else's inbox. A recent signal
  at their company (see the `signal-prospecting` skill) is the easiest source.

## One ask, low friction

- One call to action only. Two asks ("reply, or book here, or see the deck") reduce
  replies.
- A soft yes-or-no question ("Worth a look?", "Is this on your radar?") outperforms a
  hard ask ("Book a 30-minute demo").
- The first ask is for interest, not a calendar slot.

## Shapes that work

- **Observation, problem, proof, ask.** "You're doing X, which usually causes Y. We
  helped a similar company with that. Worth a look?"
- **Signal, implication, question.** "Saw you've opened a second site. That usually
  means Z gets harder. How are you handling it?"
- A longer first-touch structure, compressed into the word budget: specific context, a
  sign they have the problem, the cost of leaving it, one line of proof, a soft ask.

## Follow-up angles

Each follow-up must add something new. Options, roughly in order of use:

1. A different outcome or benefit from the one in the opener.
2. A short proof point: a result, a name the reader will recognise, a number.
3. A useful resource with no ask attached.
4. A simpler or smaller ask.
5. "Am I asking the wrong person?" to find the right contact.
6. A polite close that leaves the door open.

"Just bumping this to the top of your inbox" adds nothing and teaches the reader to
ignore you.

## Anti-patterns

- Leading with your company and a feature list instead of the reader's situation.
- Pleasantries and padding that burn the word budget before the point.
- Claims you cannot back up, or invented familiarity ("as we discussed").
- Images, attachments and multiple links in a first cold email. They hurt delivery and
  look like marketing.
