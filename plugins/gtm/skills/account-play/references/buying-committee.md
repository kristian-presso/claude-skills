# Buying committee

Complex business purchases are decided by a group, commonly reported as six to ten people.
Map the roles before contacting anyone, open with the person whose problem the product
solves most directly, and reach at least three roles before relying on the account.

## The roles

| Role | Cares about | Typical first message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic buyer | outcome, cost, risk to their targets | a business result, short |
| Champion | their own problem, looking good internally | a specific fix for their pain |
| User lead | daily workflow, effort saved | a concrete before and after |
| Technical evaluator | fit with the stack, security, effort to adopt | how it integrates, honestly |
| Procurement and legal | terms, compliance, vendor risk | nothing until asked; have the pack ready |
| Blocker | the status quo, a rival tool, their own project | address the concern; never go round them |

## Map it

1. From the product, name the team that owns the problem and the leader above it.
2. Find people in those roles from public sources; record name, role, source and how sure
   you are they still hold it.
3. Mark each with a role above. Leave gaps as gaps.
4. Pick the entry point: usually the champion or user lead closest to the pain.
5. Plan the next two threads to open once the entry point engages.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Starting at the top by default.** A cold note to a chief executive is usually
  forwarded down with no endorsement, or ignored.
- **One message for every role.** Each role cares about something different.
- **Going round a blocker.** It makes an enemy for the life of the account.
- **Guessing a role from a vague title.** "Director, Strategy" can sit anywhere; mark it
  unknown until something confirms it.

## Check

Each tier-1 account map names an entry point and two further roles, each with a source.
Before a deal is forecast, at least three roles have engaged.
